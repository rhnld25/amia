# Buffett: wonderful businesses at sensible prices

- The best investment is a business that earns high returns on the capital it employs, year after year, and can reinvest more capital at similar returns. Consistent return on equity well above the cost of capital, achieved without heavy debt, is the signature.
- A durable competitive advantage (moat) protects those returns. Common sources: brand that lets the firm charge more, switching costs, network effects, low-cost position, scale, and regulatory licences. Ask what stops a well-funded rival from taking the profits, and whether that protection is widening or narrowing.
- Stable, healthy margins through good and bad years are evidence of a moat. Margins that swing wildly suggest commodity economics.
- Owner earnings (operating cash flow minus the capital spending needed to sustain the business) are what an owner could take out. Compare their yield to the risk-free rate.
- Debt should be small relative to earnings; a good business rarely needs much of it.
- Management should allocate capital rationally: reinvest where returns are high, return cash where they are not, avoid empire-building acquisitions, and report candidly.
- A fair price for a wonderful business beats a wonderful price for a fair business, but price still matters. Overpaying turns a great company into a poor investment.
- Stay within your circle of competence. If the economics cannot be explained simply from the available facts, say so and lower confidence.
- Signs of a fading moat: falling returns on capital, margin compression from competition, rising customer churn, a key customer or product lost, or technology shifts that make the advantage irrelevant.
